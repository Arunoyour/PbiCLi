# Critical Thinking

A practical reference for thinking clearly, evaluating claims, and making sound decisions.

## What It Is

Critical thinking is the disciplined process of actively analyzing, evaluating, and synthesizing information to form a judgment, rather than accepting claims at face value or relying on gut reaction alone. It is not cynicism or constant disagreement — it's the habit of asking "how do I know this is true?" before deciding what to believe or do.

## Core Components

1. **Interpretation** — Understanding what a claim, question, or piece of information actually means before reacting to it.
2. **Analysis** — Breaking an argument down into its parts: claims, reasons, evidence, and assumptions.
3. **Evaluation** — Judging the credibility of sources and the strength of the reasoning and evidence.
4. **Inference** — Drawing reasonable conclusions from available evidence, without overreaching.
5. **Explanation** — Articulating your reasoning clearly enough that someone else could follow and check it.
6. **Self-regulation** — Monitoring your own thinking for bias, error, or unjustified certainty, and correcting course.

## Key Skills to Practice

- **Ask clarifying questions.** What exactly is being claimed? What would count as evidence for or against it?
- **Identify the argument's structure.** Separate the conclusion from the premises. Does the conclusion actually follow?
- **Check the source.** Who is making the claim, what's their expertise, and do they have an incentive to mislead?
- **Look for evidence, not just assertion.** Is the claim backed by data, a citable source, or direct observation — or just repetition and confidence?
- **Consider alternative explanations.** Before accepting the first explanation, ask what else could account for the same facts.
- **Distinguish correlation from causation.** Two things happening together doesn't mean one caused the other.
- **Seek disconfirming evidence.** Actively look for information that would prove you wrong, not just evidence that confirms what you already believe.
- **Suspend judgment when evidence is insufficient.** "I don't know yet" is a legitimate and often correct conclusion.

## Useful Frameworks

### The Socratic Method
Ask a sequence of probing questions to expose assumptions and test the logic of a position:
- What do you mean by that?
- What evidence supports this?
- Is there another way to look at this?
- What are the implications if this is true?
- Why does this matter?

### The Five Whys
Ask "why" repeatedly (typically five times) to move past surface symptoms and find a root cause rather than stopping at the first explanation.

### Steelmanning
Before critiquing a position, restate it in its strongest, most persuasive form — stronger than how your opponent may have stated it. If you can't argue the other side convincingly, you don't yet understand it well enough to critique it.

### The Toulmin Model (for evaluating arguments)
Break an argument into:
- **Claim** — what is being asserted
- **Grounds** — the evidence or data behind it
- **Warrant** — the reasoning connecting grounds to claim
- **Backing** — support for the warrant itself
- **Qualifier** — how certain the claim is (always, usually, sometimes)
- **Rebuttal** — conditions under which the claim wouldn't hold

## Common Cognitive Biases to Watch For

| Bias | What It Is |
|---|---|
| Confirmation bias | Favoring information that confirms existing beliefs, ignoring what contradicts them |
| Anchoring bias | Over-relying on the first piece of information encountered |
| Availability heuristic | Judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind, not actual frequency |
| Bandwagon effect | Believing something because many other people believe it |
| Sunk cost fallacy | Continuing a course of action because of past investment, not future value |
| Dunning-Kruger effect | Overestimating competence in areas of limited knowledge |
| Motivated reasoning | Reaching conclusions that align with what you want to be true |
| Survivorship bias | Drawing conclusions only from visible "survivors," ignoring the failures that went unseen |

## Spotting Weak Arguments (Logical Fallacies)

- **Ad hominem** — attacking the person instead of the argument
- **Straw man** — misrepresenting an argument to make it easier to attack
- **False dichotomy** — presenting only two options when more exist
- **Appeal to authority** — treating a claim as true solely because an authority said so, without evidence
- **Slippery slope** — assuming one step inevitably leads to an extreme, unlikely outcome
- **Circular reasoning** — using the conclusion as its own supporting premise

## A Practical Checklist

Before accepting a claim or making a decision, ask:

- [ ] What exactly is being claimed?
- [ ] What evidence supports it, and how strong is that evidence?
- [ ] Who is the source, and what is their incentive?
- [ ] What assumptions am I making — and are they justified?
- [ ] What would change my mind?
- [ ] What's the strongest counterargument, and have I addressed it?
- [ ] Am I reasoning toward the truth, or toward a conclusion I already want?
- [ ] What's the cost of being wrong, and does my confidence match the evidence?

## Why It Matters

Critical thinking doesn't guarantee correct answers, but it reduces the odds of being manipulated, misled, or simply wrong due to lazy reasoning. It's a habit built through consistent practice — questioning claims (including your own), checking sources, and staying open to being wrong.
